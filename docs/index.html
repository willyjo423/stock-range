The watch column. Every range on this page is drawn outward from today's close, so today sits in the middle of all of them by construction — asking where a stock is inside its own bracket has the same answer for all five hundred. Ranking needs a second reference point, and this uses two.

Where available, where today's price sits inside a range published earlier and still running. A stock at the 92nd percentile of the range forecast for it three weeks ago has moved further than expected, which is the truest reading of "at the edge of its bracket". This fills in as the daily runs accumulate.

Otherwise, expected turbulence: the near-term band against the long-term one, both annualised so they compare. A stock whose next week is priced far wider than its next quarter is one the model thinks is about to move.

The arrows. ▲ and ▼ mark whether the forecast median for that window sits above or below the day's typical stock. Every stock drifts upward over a quarter because the market does, and that part is identical for all five hundred names and useless — so the arrow shows only what is left after the crowd is subtracted. ● means in line with everything else.

Treat them as the weakest thing on the page, and here is exactly how weak. Every training run measures them, and the last one found the cross-sectional tilt called direction correctly 51.5% of the time at one week — 1.5 points better than a coin, and 51.4% over a month, on half a million independent windows.

Two things make that smaller than it looks. Those windows share dates with one another, so the confidence figure printed in the run is flattering. And 1.3 points is 1.3 points. What the same measurement found alongside it matters more: without the cross-sectional subtraction the tilt is actively wrong — 53.7% against a base rate of 54.4% for simply always saying up. The subtraction is not a refinement, it is the entire effect. Had the page shown the raw tilt, it would have pointed the wrong way.

So the arrows stay, because a measured 1.3 points is not zero. They are not a reason to buy anything.

Nothing here says which way with confidence. The watch column says a stock already moved or is likely to move; the arrows are a faint tilt. Green and red mark up and down, not good and bad.

A calibrated range is not a trading strategy. It says what is plausible, not what is mispriced.
